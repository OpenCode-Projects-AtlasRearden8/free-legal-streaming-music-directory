# Archives for Older Music

Recordings that are historical, long out of copyright, or have been released as public domain by their owners. Nobody's selling anything here: these are libraries and archives actually putting the past online for free.

## Internet Archive

**[archive.org/details/audio](https://archive.org/details/audio)**

A deep ocean of legal free audio: live concert archives (built on each band's own taping policy), historical collections, radio programs, spoken word, and decades of donated audio. One URL family, millions of items, all free-streaming right in your browser.

*Where to start:* the audio department indexes "collections" down the left side of the page, and the two best for music lovers are right below this line.

### The 78 RPM Records collection

**[archive.org/details/78rpm](https://archive.org/details/78rpm)**

Over 400,000 digitized shellac records from roughly 1898 to the 1950s — jazz, blues, opera, comedy, old radio spots — digitized through a big archivist collaboration (the Great 78 Project). If you've ever wondered what the Gramophone era actually sounded like, this is your education. The sound is crackly and utterly unique.

**The Fine Print:**
- Rights vary record by record — pre-1924 recordings are solidly public domain in the US, later ones are mostly free to *listen* but not always free to *reuse*. Treat this as a listening archive unless you clear rights for a commercial project.
- Streaming is easy; FLAC downloads are large.

### Live Music Archive (etree)

**[archive.org/details/etree](https://archive.org/details/etree)**

Band-approved concert recordings — literally thousands of Grateful Dead and related live shows plus hundreds of other bands who explicitly allow audience taping. Full shows, free to stream.

**The Fine Print:**
- Available-to-stream vs. available-to-download depends on *each band's* policy — some allow downloads (Dead, notably), some only streaming (many indie bands chose "stream only" after 2005).
- Soundboard-vs-audience quality varies wildly; read the notes.

## Library of Congress National Jukebox

**[loc.gov/collections/national-jukebox](https://www.loc.gov/collections/national-jukebox/)**

The Library of Congress streaming tens of thousands of historical recordings (Victor, Columbia, and Edison-era discs dating from 1901 to 1925) with a real streaming player and museum-grade metadata — producers, dates, and liner details for each item. This is the "I want to hear the story behind the song" archive.

**The Fine Print:**
- Streaming only — the Jukebox plays selections but doesn't offer downloads, because their rights agreements are built around listening.
- The player is a genuine archive tool, not a slick modern app. The charm is part of the experience.

## IMSLP / Petrucci Music Library

**[imslp.org/wiki/Main_Page](https://imslp.org/wiki/Main_Page)**

The great public-domain music library: hundreds of thousands of public-domain sheet music scores plus a large collection of recordings of those works, contributed from around the world. If "older music" means classical, IMSLP is deep, searchable, and free.

*How to start:* browse scores from the main page and pick a composer; recordings sit alongside each score. Downloads pause behind a short "please consider supporting us" wait screen — a fair trade, since it's free.

**The Fine Print:**
- Licensing is country-specific: some recordings are marked "USA-only" vs. "worldwide" depending on where the recording's copyright has expired. Check the flag label before downloading.
- IMSLP shows ads to free users; a modest paid subscription removes them. We list it here because the free tier genuinely works.
